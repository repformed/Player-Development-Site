Lesson 01 · Read before you react

How to read a football diagram.

A diagram is a shorthand explanation. Start with its key rather than assuming every coach uses the same shapes and arrows. In this example, the circle marked P represents a player. The arrow shows where that player is planned to move.

Next, find the reference lines. The line of scrimmage marks where the play starts; the line to gain marks the distance the offense needs for a new first down. They describe different things.

An arrow is a plan, not a result. It does not show speed, contact technique, what a defender will do, or whether the play succeeds. If a symbol or responsibility is missing, ask instead of supplying it from memory.

Use a three-part explanation.

  1. Name the key: “P is a player, and the arrow means planned movement.”
  2. Name the reference: “The dashed line is the line of scrimmage.”
  3. Name the limit: “The drawing does not tell me how fast to move.”

Lesson 02 · Make the numbers useful

Downs and distance, without the guesswork.

The down tells you which attempt the offense is on. The distance tells you how many yards remain to reach the line to gain. In an ordinary series, the offense has up to four downs to earn the next first down, normally by advancing ten yards. Goal-to-go situations and penalties need additional context.

2nd & 6

Read both parts.

Second down means the second attempt in the series. Six means six yards remain to the line to gain. It does not mean six yards were gained on the last play.

GAIN 4 → 3rd & 2

Update the situation.

If the play ends after a four-yard gain, with no penalty, turnover or first down, one down has been used and two yards remain: 6 − 4 = 2.

If the offense reaches the line to gain, the result is a new first down, not simply the next numbered down with zero yards left. This lesson isolates the arithmetic; clock, field position, penalties and the rules of your competition can change decisions.

Take the idea with you

Ask a better football question.

When you next look at a team diagram, first ask “What does this key mean?” Then ask “What is the down and distance?” Those questions separate the drawing from the situation. A parent or teammate can listen to your explanation without needing to grade physical technique.
