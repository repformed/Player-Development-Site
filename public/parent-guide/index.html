Four different needs

Match the support to the gap.

01 / UNDERSTANDING

“I don’t know what that means.”

Start with rules, vocabulary and a simple diagram. Ask the learner to explain one idea in their own words. The proposed Foundation package focuses on football IQ and quizzes, with no drills.

Try downs and distance
02 / GUIDED PRACTICE

“I understand it, but need to learn the movement.”

A text explanation is not the same as a demonstration or supervised practice. The proposed Fundamentals package adds movement sessions; these articles do not provide a workout or certify technique.

Understand the proposed learning path
03 / POSITION KNOWLEDGE

“How does my role fit the play?”

Separate a position label from an assignment. A team’s call and rules matter. Position Development proposes a selected-position pathway and additional drills; a general article cannot replace the team playbook.

04 / INDIVIDUAL FEEDBACK

“I need someone to look at my work.”

That requires actual footage and a coach’s assessment, not an automatic quiz result. Elite proposes two corrections per athlete per month; it is not available to purchase through this guide.

A higher tier is not a substitute for the right starting point. If the athlete cannot explain the vocabulary yet, the free reading lessons offer a useful first conversation.

Experience matters alongside age

Change the conversation as the learner develops.

  • New players, including new adults: begin with a single term and a simple example. Do not assume that age means someone already knows downs, positions or a diagram’s key.
  • Youth and middle-school learners: use the team coach’s words and ask for a short explanation back. Check what supervision and permission would be required before considering any future online participation or practice service.
  • High-school players: bring the conversation closer to their actual team responsibilities. “Which part of our call changes this assignment?” is more useful than memorizing another team’s terminology.
  • Experienced or returning players: identify what has changed—position, scheme or the question they are trying to answer—rather than treating every lesson as a fresh start.

These are ways to discuss learning, not age-based exercise prescriptions. Eligibility, supervision and individual readiness cannot be determined from a public webpage.

Set realistic expectations

What online learning cannot see.

A correct reading answer shows understanding of that question. It does not prove that an athlete can execute a technique under pressure. A short video also has limits: camera angle, what happens outside the frame and missing play context can change what a coach can assess.

Keep direct team coaching and appropriate supervision central. Do not use these non-contact reading lessons to improvise tackling, blocking, strength work or a return-to-play decision. Questions about health or injury belong with qualified healthcare professionals, not a subscription quiz.

Look for a clear description of what a service includes, who provides feedback and what it cannot promise. No online plan can guarantee playing time, recruiting, a roster spot or individual results.

Before you express interest

Bring the question. Keep personal details private.

  1. Write one learning need in ordinary language: vocabulary, guided practice, a position question or individual feedback.
  2. Compare that need with the proposed packages and prices, including which benefits are inherited from lower levels.
  3. Read Devyn Billiott and Victor Bennett’s biographies and ask about the scope you need.
  4. Use an adult contact email. Do not put child names, birth dates, health details or personal videos into the interest form.

Expressing interest does not enroll anyone, reserve a place or take payment. There is no reason to submit sensitive athlete information at this stage.
