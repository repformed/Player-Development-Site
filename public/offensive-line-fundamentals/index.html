Offensive-line fundamentals · Classroom first

Know your place.
Understand your job.

Offensive-line learning begins before physical technique: name the positions, understand the play’s purpose and know which assignment questions belong to your coach.

A non-contact reading lesson. This page does not prescribe stance, leverage, collisions or blocking technique.

The key distinction

Position names a role in the line.
Alignment describes where someone is set.
Assignment describes what the called play requires.

One picture cannot supply all three.

Lesson 01 · Learn the vocabulary

Read the five line positions.

The familiar five-player offensive line has a center in the middle, guards immediately to either side and tackles outside the guards. Name left and right from the offense’s perspective; a diagram facing the other direction can otherwise be confusing.

The center handles the snap, but the exact communication and identification responsibilities depend on the team. Do not turn a position name into a universal rule about whom that player blocks.

The diagram isolates the line so you can learn the labels. It omits the other offensive players, defenders, formation and play call. You cannot judge a complete formation’s legality or a blocking assignment from this row alone.

Lesson 02 · Replace guessing with a process

How to think about a blocking assignment.

Seeing a defender directly in front of a player does not, by itself, prove that defender is the player’s assignment. The called run or protection, the defensive picture and your team’s rules must be understood together. This lesson gives you questions to ask, not a blocking system to execute.

01 / THE CALL

What is the play trying to do?

Ask your coach to explain the run concept or protection and the words that identify it. Repeat the purpose back in your own words. Do not borrow a term from another team and assume it means the same thing.

02 / THE PICTURE

What information is shown?

Identify your position and the defenders your coach wants you to recognize. If the drawing omits a defender or changes the front, identify the missing information rather than guessing a response.

03 / THE RULE

Which team rule applies?

Ask “Under this call, what makes this my responsibility?” A useful explanation connects the call to the assignment instead of saying “always block the person in front.”

04 / THE CHECK

What would change the answer?

Ask your coach who communicates a change and what you should clarify when the picture differs. Use a classroom explanation; do not improvise contact practice from a webpage.

A useful question for your coach

“What changes my responsibility?”

Bring one diagram and one precise question, such as “I can identify the right guard here, but I do not know which part of our call sets the responsibility.” That gives your coach a clear place to start. Parents can help by listening for a reason rather than treating a guessed answer as mastery.
